\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}
One heavily relies on publicly available data.
In the context of e-commerce, for example, this can be data like competitor pricing, sales trends, or market statistics.
Querying them directly may reveal sensitive information about interests and focus areas of the querying party.
Competitors could evaluate these queries and deduct information about the party of interest.
Solutions for hiding access patterns are required.

The most trivial solution is to hide access patterns by accessing the database in its whole.
This approach is known as \textit{Linear Scan} \cite{doerner2017scale}.
Using this technique for large databases, however, is inefficient due to its high costs of bandwidth and storage.

\textit{Oblivious Random Access Memory (ORAM)} has the primary objective of obfuscating memory access patterns \cite{vadapalli2023duoram} while providing linear or even sublinear runtime and communication.
This research primarily focuses on \textit{DUORAM}, an \textit{ORAM} implementation by Vadapalli et al. \cite{vadapalli2023duoram}.
It, too, hides access patterns while implementing read and write operations.
This is achieved by sharing the database either additively or via XOR between two non-colluding parties $P_0,P_1$. 
Read and write accesses are then executed such that each server on its own cannot find out which element was retrieved or written to and what the element was.
Combining both results, again via XOR or addition, returns the requested database entry.
Protocols and implementations exist for two and three-parties.
Introducing a third auxiliary party $P_2$, which does not hold any data but merely facilitates the communication, eliminates the need for a trusted party by enabling \textit{(2+1)-Party Multi Party Computation ((2+1)-Party MPC)} protocols.

\textit{DUORAM} is characterized by its two phases.
The \textit{Preprocessing Phase} generates \textit{Distributed Point Functions (DPFs)}, \textit{DUORAM}'s most important concept, on random target indices.
These serve as accessing-tools in the \textit{Online-Phase}, which includes the database accesses and results in retrieving or writing to the desired element.
Again, combining both shared databases held by $P_0$ and $P_1$ results in the initial database.

Vadapalli et al. implemented a basic solution for \textit{DUORAM}.
Their implementation offers functionality for storing, retrieving and updating 64-bit values.
This research aims to extend this implementation for values of arbitrary size resulting in the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What approaches exist, and what are their distinctive features in terms of concepts and implementation?
    \item How do these approaches perform in terms of general performance metrics such as communication-speed and storage-requirements?
    \item Are there any general concepts or datatypes that prove particularly useful in terms of scalability?
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Roadmap}\label{sec:roadmap}
To answer these questions, the research is structured as follows:
\ref{ch:TechnicalFoundations} lays the mathematical and conceptual fundamentals for understanding \textit{ORAM} and \textit{DUORAM} introduced in \ref{ch:ORAM}.
\ref{ch:Implementation} builds upon these theoretical introductions to further dive into the theoretical reading- and updating-protocols used in \textit{DUORAM}.
Starting from there, an initial theoretical scaling-approach is outlined.
The same chapter will then introduce the given implementation by Vadapalli et al. including first executions and scenarios.
This implementation will then be expanded for each scaling-approach.
Finally, \ref{ch:Evaluation} executes benchmarks on each approach while deriving performance metrics.
These metrics are based on bandwidth, entry-size, communication efficiency, and storage-efficiency.
Each approach is tested, benchmarked and compared with its fellow-approaches based on these metrics.
The results are then used in \ref{ch:Conclusion} to answer the research questions and highlights unanswered questions as well as potential approaches for further research in the future.

\section*{Related Work}\label{sec:relatedWorks}
This section introduces further research the benevolent reader might be interested in.
It is, however, not required to read all of it for understanding this research.

\textit{DUORAM} extends the concept of \textit{FLORAM}, another \textit{ORAM} implementation, introduced by Doerner et al. \cite{doerner2017scale}.
Main concepts such as protocols, the base-implementation, and definitions of \textit{DUORAM} are taken by Vadapalli et al. \cite{vadapalli2023duoram}.
As already mentioned in the introduction and further extended in \ref{sub:DistributedPointFunctions}, both \textit{ORAM} approaches use \textit{DPFs} as their main tool for database accesses.
These were initially introduced by Boyle et al. \cite{boyle2015function,boyle2021function,boyle2023information,boyle2016function} including definitions on \textit{Functions Secret Sharing (FSS)} and \textit{Multi Party Computation (MPC)}.
\textit{FSS} and \textit{MPC} are further explained in \ref{sub:BeaverTriples} and \ref{ch_FunctionSecretSharing}.
