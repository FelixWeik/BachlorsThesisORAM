\subsection{Comparison Functions}
\label{sub:ComparisonFunctions}
Comparison Functions act like Point Functions (\ref{sub:PointFunctions}) just for intervals.
This means:
\[
f^{<}_{\alpha}(i) = \begin{cases} 
      1 & \text{if } i < \alpha, \\
      0 & \text{else}.
   \end{cases}
\]

Recall the binary tree implementation of point functions.
Please note, the following implementation will only work if the leaves are placed in ascending order.
This means, the smallest leaf is on the left while the biggest leaf is on the right.
In our implementation, the leafs will present indices with the leaf on the left representing index $0$ and the rightmost leaf representing index $2^n$ (assuming a tree of depth $n$).

\subsection{Distributed Comparison Functions (DCF)}
\label{sub:DistributedComparisonFunctions}
The function secret sharing scheme (\ref{ch_FunctionSecretSharing}) for secretly sharing Comparison Functions is called \textit{Distributed Comparison Function (DCF)}.
On a high level, both shares are represented by binary trees.
If one evaluates the function, she traverses the tree (\ref{sub:TraversingTheDPFTree}).
In simple point functions (\ref{sub:PointFunctions}), one is just interested in the flag bits, correction words (including the final correction word), and the label of the leaf.
Now, however, one is interested, whether she stays on the path or not while traversing the tree.
Since the leafs are places in ascending order, leaving the path to leaf $\alpha$ to the left implicates that the given value is smaller than threshold $\alpha$.
Thus, the function will evaluate to $1$.
Leaving the path to $\alpha$ to the right or staying on the path to alpha with a given input $i$ implicates $i\geq\alpha$.
The function will evaluate to $0$.

\subsection{DCF Construction}
\label{sub:CDPFConstruction}
Multiple DCF construction coexist.
This research focuses on the construction by Kyle et al. \cite{kyle2023grotto}.
Goal of this construction is to find out, whether a shared secret $x$ is greater or less than zero.
The benevolent reader might also be interested in the more prominent DCF construction of Boyle et al. \cite{boyle2021function}.

Kyle et al. \cite{kyle2023grotto} are more favorable in their communication cost and ease of implementation.
Therefore, this construction will be used in our own implementation.
For this purpose they use a pair of the already mentioned DPFs (\ref{sub:DistributedPointFunctions}), and expand them to meet the functional requirements of DCFs.
Also, calculating the result only requires one single word to be exchanged.

First, both DPFs are generated on the same, random target value.
For ease of explanation, we will take a look at a construction using a single DPF first and evaluate on 64-bit signed integers only.
Both parties $P_0,P_1$ will hold a share of the random target value $target_0, target_1$ with $target = target_0+target_1$.
Given a secret-shared input $x$ with $x=x_0+x_1$ each party calculates $target_i-x_i$.
Both parties exchange this value.
This allows them to reconstruct $S=target-x=(target_0-x_0)+(target_1-x_1)$.

To simplify the explanation consider a bit-vector $V$ with length $2^{64}$.
It is populated with 1s in the range of $[S,S+2^{63}-1]$ and 0s everywhere else.
This corresponds to the partitioning of the two's complement for a 64-bit signed integer.
Let us assume the result of the DPF to be the dot-product of this vector $V$ and the DPF.
This means, 0 everywhere but the target-index of the DPF.

If we now evaluate the DPF on $target$, which is equal of calculating $V[target]$, we will receive a value of either $0$ or $1$.
Assume $V[target]=1$.
This means $target = S+k$ for some $k\in [1,2^{63}-1]$.
We conclude that $x>0$ since $S$ is shifted to the left and therefore $V$ will evaluate to $0$.
If $V[target]=0$ we conclude $x<=0$ since $S=target-(-x) = target + x$ which will shift the target into the $0$ area of $V$.
